13. Labelling, QR codes and battery passports ​

This section explains the common rules for physical labels, markings, QR codes and battery passports. These are distinct mechanisms. A QR code is a physical data carrier, not a battery passport, and does not replace physical markings or CE marking.

13.1 Physical labels and markings ​

Article 13 creates duties with different scopes and dates. Manufacturers ensure Article 13 labelling for batteries they place on the market or put into service (Article 38(1)); importers and distributors have verification duties (Articles 41(2) and 42(2)).

ItemScope and ruleTiming or qualification
General labelAll batteries; Annex VI, Part A (Article 13(1))From 18 August 2026 or 18 months after entry into force of the Article 13(10) act, whichever is later; OQ-REG-001 remains open.
Capacity labelRechargeable portable, LMT and SLI batteries (Article 13(2))Same conditional start rule.
Duration and “non-rechargeable” labelsNon-rechargeable portable batteries (Article 13(3))Same conditional start rule.
Separate-collection symbolAll batteries (Article 13(4); Annex VI, Part B)From 18 August 2025; size and small-symbol packaging rules apply.
Cd or Pb symbolBatteries exceeding 0.002% cadmium or 0.004% lead (Article 13(5))Printed beneath the collection symbol; see OQ-REG-002 on transition.

The general label includes manufacturer identity; battery category and Article 38(6) identification; place and date of manufacture; weight; capacity; chemistry; specified hazardous substances; usable extinguishing agent; and critical raw materials above 0.1% by weight (Annex VI, Part A). Article 38(6) requires model identification with a batch or serial number, product number or another identifying element; it does not universally require serialisation.

Article 13(7) requires the Article 13(1)–(6) labels and QR code to be visible, legible and indelible. Specified circular operations require new labels or markings and QR access to status-change information (Article 13(9); Annex XIII, point 4); the non-passport implementation is unresolved under OQ-REG-007.

13.2 Physical CE marking ​

CE marking and QR access are separate. Article 20(1) requires CE marking visibly, legibly and indelibly on the battery or, where impossible or not warranted, on packaging and accompanying documents. It is affixed before placing on the market or putting into service (Article 20(2)); a notified-body number follows where Annex VIII requires it (Article 20(3)).

The declaration of conformity is QR-accessible under Article 13(6)(b) and Annex XIII, point 1(r). That access does not replace physical CE marking under Articles 19–20, and CE marking does not satisfy the QR duty.

13.3 The regulatory QR code requirement ​

From 18 February 2027 all batteries must bear a QR code (Article 13(6)). Annex VI, Part C requires high contrast and a size readable by a commonly available reader; Article 13(7) governs permanence and placement.

Battery CategoryReferenceThe role of QR code
LMT; industrial above 2 kWh; EVArticle 13(6)(a)Leads to battery passport
Other battery, including industrial at exactly 2 kWh or lessArticle 13(6)(b)Leads to applicable labels, declaration, report and other information.
SLIArticle 13(6)(b) and (c)Leads to “other battery” information plus applicable recycled-content information.

13.4 QR codes for batteries not requiring a battery passport ​

For batteries not requiring a battery passport the regulation requires access to applicable information, declaration, reports and waste information and that the information must be complete, up-to-date and accurate.

QR code encoding the GS1 Digital Link URI with GTIN + batch/lot number leads to required level of information.

13.5 QR codes for batteries requiring a battery passport ​

For batteries that require a battery passport, the battery passport shall be accessible through the QR code which encodes a unique identifier (GS1 Digital Link) that the economic operator placing the battery on the market shall print or engrave on the battery.

The QR code and the unique identifier shall comply with the ISO/IEC standards 15459, or equivalent. GS1 identification standards comply with ISO/IEC 15459. QR codes encoding the GS1 Digital Link URI with GTIN + serial number leads to the battery passport of that individual battery.

13.6 QR code placement options ​

The labels and the QR code shall be printed or engraved visibly, legibly and indelibly on the battery. Where this is not possible or not warranted on account of the nature and size of the battery, the labels and the QR code shall be affixed to the packaging and to the documents accompanying the battery.

13.7 GS1 Digital Link URI ​

GS1 provides a way to encode a unique identifier in a QR code and makes the information web accessible. A GS1 Digital Link is a URI encoding a GTIN and qualifiers including batch/lot and/or serial number.

The Regulation requires the battery passport or the battery information being accessible via QR code. GS1 Digital Link supports this functionality in an interoperable and globally unique manner. The standard is used as follows:

  1. Determine the identified object and model, batch or instance granularity. Keep an incorporated or supplied battery distinct from its host product.
  2. Allocate GTIN to the battery model (trade item) and add the appropriate qualifier (batch/lot or serial number) where finer granularity is required.
  3. Construct the URI in accordance with Section 4, “GS1 Digital Link URI syntax” (pp. 19–36), of the GS1 Digital Link URI Syntax standard.
  4. Encode this URI syntax in a QR code according to GS1 rules, meeting Article 13(7) and Annex VI, Part C.
  5. Redirect public requests to correct resources.
  6. Keep the GS1 identifier permanently associated with the same battery or battery model. Even if the information, service provider, website or resolver changes, do not reassign the identifier to another object.

This provides globally unique identification and a stable entry point.

13.9 Multiple audiences and information resources ​

Scannig a QR code can direct to several resources depending on the audiences and their access levels. For example, a product landing page can expose public resources; access-based resources can serve authorities, notified bodies and legitimate-interest users.
