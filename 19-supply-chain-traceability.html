19. Supply-chain traceability and information exchange ​

19.1 Regulatory traceability functions ​

The battery regulation expresses the importance of traceability stating that “ Ensuring traceability of a battery throughout the whole supply chain helps to make market surveillance simpler and more efficient, and provides transparency to consumers. An efficient traceability system facilitates the market surveillance authorities’ task of tracing economic operators who have placed on the market, made available on the market or put into service non-compliant batteries. Economic operators should therefore be required to keep information on their transactions involving batteries for a certain period, including in electronic form.” (Paragraph 76)

In addition Article 46 states that

  1. Economic operators shall, upon a request of a national authority, provide the following information to the market surveillance authorities: (a) the identity of any economic operator that has supplied them with a battery; (b) the identity of any economic operator to which they have supplied a battery, as well as the quantity and exact models.
  2. Economic operators shall ensure that they are able to provide the information referred to in paragraph 1 for 10 years after they have been supplied with the battery and for 10 years after they have been supplied with the battery and for 10 years after they have supplied the battery.

Beyond this article 49 requires companies to know, document, manage and communicate where critical battery raw materials come from, how they move through the supply chain, and how related social and environmental risks are controlled. Article 49(1) D requires economic operators to have a traceability system in place;

” establish and operate a system of controls and transparency regarding the supply chain, including a chain of custody or traceability system, identifying upstream actors in the supply chain”

The article further explains that traceability system must also be supported by documentation on: • The raw material’s description, trade name and type. • Supplier name and address. • Country of origin and transaction history from extraction to the immediate supplier. • Quantity of raw material in the battery. • Third-party verification reports. • Additional OECD-based information for materials from conflict-affected or high-risk areas, if verification reports are unavailable.

GS1 has standards in place that can help economic operators to establish and maintain traceability systems to meet the requirements and enable the different parties to document and share relevant information.

See Section 14, “Battery due diligence”, for due-diligence supply-chain controls and Section 13, “Labelling, QR codes and battery passports”, for the passport and its access rules. Neither substitutes for Article 46 records.

19.2 Establish and operate a system of controls and transparency for the raw-material supply chain ​

Economic operators are required to know and document where critical raw materials come from and how they move through the supply chain. (Article 49).

For Article 46, a minimal transfer record needs to preserve the relationship between the supplying and receiving economic operators, exact battery model, quantity supplied and the retention periods triggered by receipt and supply. Transaction date, document reference, ship-from and ship-to location, lot or serial identifier, logistic-unit identifier and event time may aid retrieval, reconciliation and recall, but Article 46 does not expressly require those additional fields.

Designing, establishing and maintaining a traceability system can be supported by implementing two key elements: Key Data Elements, which capture the who, where, what, why and when a product is transformed or changes location or ownership, and Critical Tracking Events, such as raw material extraction, transformation, processing, receipt and shipment. By defining what information must be captured and at which points in the supply chain, this approach makes it easier to establish, maintain and scale a traceability system across different actors and processes.

In GS1, the who and where are identified using a Global Location number, GLN while the what can be identified using GTIN and the different levels of granularity, GTIN plus batch/Lot and or plus serial number. For logistics units, the Serial Shipping Container Code SSCC can be used to identify the “what”.

EPCIS (electronic product code information service) is GS1 standard for creating and sharing event-based traceability information.

With EPCIS, organisations can: • Share information about the physical movements and status of products as they move through the supply chain. • Answer key traceability questions: What happened? Who? Where? When? Why? • Implement a traceability database (EPCIS repository) as part of their IT architecture. • Make data available through requests or subscriptions between parties in the ecosystem.

The GS1 Core Business Vocabulary (CBV) complements EPCIS by providing a standardised set of terms and definitions for describing supply chain events in a consistent way. By using CBV together with EPCIS, organizations can ensure that traceability data is recorded, shared and interpreted consistently across systems and trading partners. This reduces ambiguity, improves interoperability and makes event-based traceability information easier to exchange and understand.

19.3 Identify the battery with GTIN ​

Article 46 requires economic operators to be able to identify the exact battery models supplied or received, but does not prescribe GTIN. Where the battery is managed as a trade item, an implementation may assign it a GTIN. GTIN identifies the battery model or trade item; GTIN plus batch/lot number identifies a batch/lot, and GTIN plus serial number identifies an individual battery. A GTIN alone does not identify an individual instance.

In the traceability approach introduced in Section 19.2, these identifiers provide the “what”; Section 19.4 explains how GLN may identify the relevant economic operators and locations. Detailed guidance on battery identification, identification granularity, batteries incorporated in or supplied with other products, and GTIN management appears in Sections 12.2–12.7, “Battery and grouping identification”. Further applications are described throughout the category-specific and common implementation sections and illustrated in Section 20, “Worked examples”.

19.4 Identify economic operator with GLN ​

For Article 46, the identity that must be retrievable is the economic operator that supplied or received the battery. An implementation should distinguish the legal entity or other party, the organisational function, the physical location and, where relevant, the digital location. These are different identification scopes. A legal entity may operate several functions and locations, and several parties may act at one location

Global Location Identifier (GLN) can be used to identify a legal entity, a functional, physical location or digital location making. An implementation may use a Global Location Number (GLN) for interoperable identification. GLN identifies a legal entity, party, function, physical location or digital location.

For Article 46, an implementation may use a GLN as a party key linked to the name, address and evidence establishing the economic operator's identity. Separate GLNs identify the legal entity, ordering function, ship-from location and ship-to location where needed. The GLN Data Model can describe organisations, places, addresses, contacts and status history (GS1 Global Location Number Data Model Solution Standard, sections 4–5, pp. 11–50).

A GLN does not replace an authority-issued producer or EPR registration number, company registration number, customs identifier or other legally issued identifier. The record may link them but shall preserve the official identifier and issuing context.

19.5 CTE: Shipping and receiving ​

Shipping and receiving records can create the downstream and upstream links needed for Article 46. The parties should use compatible identifiers and reconcile battery model, quantity and, where used, batch, serial or logistic-unit identifiers. An implementation may use an SSCC for a pallet or case. SSCC identifies the logistic unit; it does not identify its batteries unless the content relationship is also recorded. See Section 12.5, “Logistic-unit identification using SSCC”.

Where EPCIS is used, shipping and receiving may be separate observations using the appropriate event structure, business step, location, source or destination and transaction references. The implementation should define how discrepancies, partial receipts, returns and reading errors are corrected. A dispatch event recorded by one party is not, by itself, evidence that the other received the same quantity.

Importers and distributors shall ensure that storage or transport conditions under their responsibility do not jeopardise conformity (Articles 41(4) and 42(4)); fulfilment service providers have the corresponding Article 43 duty. EPCIS can carry condition information where needed, but the Regulation does not impose continuous monitoring or require EPCIS (GS1 EPCIS Standard, version 2.0.1, sections 7.3.5–7.3.7, pp. 56–73).

19.6 CTE: Transformation and battery assembly ​

Battery manufacture can involve cells, modules, packs, electronic components and materials. The legal and operational need determines whether records cover only the finished model, an input batch, individual inputs or detailed genealogy. Article 39 requires cell and module suppliers to provide manufacturers, free of charge, information and documentation necessary for compliance.

Where input-to-output genealogy is needed, an implementation may use GS1 identification structures. GTIN identifies trade-item classes, GTIN plus batch or lot number identifies batches, and GTIN plus serial number identifies instances. See Section 12.6, “Choosing the appropriate identification level”.

An EPCIS TransformationEvent can express relationships between identified inputs and outputs (GS1 EPCIS Standard, version 2.0.1, section 7.4.5, pp. 93–96). It should be used only where the process is a transformation within the standard. The record does not establish conformity or replace technical documentation, assessment, testing or verification.

Module suppliers can make information and documentation necessary for compliance by sharing relevant information, for example by sharing a GS1 Digital link that provides manufacturers with access to the information.

19.7 CTE: Association of batteries with host products ​

Where a battery is incorporated in or supplied with another product, the battery and host product remain distinct identification and data objects. Battery attributes should describe the battery and host-product attributes the host. See Sections 12.3, “Battery incorporated in another trade item”, and 12.4, “Battery supplied with another trade item”.

The relationship may be recorded at model, batch or instance level. A model compatibility relationship differs from a record that a particular serialised battery was installed in a particular serialised vehicle; the latter requires valid instance identifiers for both. If only the host product is referenced in a transaction, the system needs to retain a resolvable link to the battery model and quantity where that link is relied upon for Article 46.

EPCIS AggregationEvent and AssociationEvent support different parent-child relationships (GS1 EPCIS Standard, version 2.0.1, sections 7.4.3 and 7.4.6, pp. 83–88 and 96–102). Select the event from the actual relationship and full EPCIS semantics

19.8 Share the right information at the right time ​

Traceability data is generated from various sources and at different stages throughout the product lifecycle. This data can be classified into several categories. Ensuring that the appropriate information is shared at the right time is essential for effective traceability. According to GS1 standards, business data shared between end users falls into three main categories: master data, transaction data, and event data.

Master data: Descriptive attributes for objects, locations, and parties identified with GS1 identifiers. For example static data about GTIN for products and GLN for locations and parties. For those using GDSN GTIN master data is usually shared with GDSN. Location and party masterdata can be registered and shared using GS1 Registry platforms.

Transaction data: Trade transactions that trigger or confirm the execution of activities within business processes, from order to completion. For example: Orders, transactions, deliveries, and invoices. In GS1 this data is shared with Electronic Data Interchange EDI. Otherwise, this information is usually shared with paper documents supplied with a shipment.

Event data: Describes where an object is, when it happens and why, throughout the supply chain. Allows you to track trade items in real-time. In GS1 this data is shared with EPCIS.

19.9 EPCIS event data ​

EPCIS is a GS1 standard for capturing and sharing visibility event data. Its dimensions can describe what objects participated, when and where the event occurred, why it occurred in the business process and, where relevant, how sensor observations were made (GS1 EPCIS Standard, version 2.0.1, sections 6.1 and 7.2.2, pp. 33–36 and 47–51).

Potential uses include commissioning identifiers, production or inspection observations, logistic-unit aggregation, shipping and receiving, battery-host association, transformation, repair, replacement and end-of-life processes. GS1 Core Business Vocabulary (CBV) provides vocabulary and identifier syntax used with EPCIS (GS1 Core Business Vocabulary Standard, sections 3 and 7–9, pp. 21–29 and 34–123).

A CBV term resembling a regulated activity does not mean the Regulation requires it or that the event proves lawful performance. Implementers should apply complete EPCIS and CBV definitions and govern battery-specific user vocabulary.

19.10 How EPCIS is used ​

An EPCIS implementation should:

  1. identify the regulatory function or operational need, actor, battery categories and period;
  2. select model, batch, instance, packaging or logistic-unit granularity, without using GTIN alone for an individual battery;
  3. identify relevant parties, functions and locations, using GLNs where appropriate;
  4. define CTEs and KDEs, including quantity, corrections, returns, splits, aggregation and transformation;
  5. map processes to EPCIS event types and CBV vocabulary after checking their complete definitions;
  6. define capture, validation, storage, query, authentication, authorisation and retention; and
  7. test that records answer the relevant request, including Article 46 throughout its ten-year periods.

EPCIS supports capture and query interfaces and access control and redaction (GS1 EPCIS Standard, version 2.0.1, sections 8.1–8.2, pp. 103–145). It does not require one database. Parties should agree identifier resolution, sharing, authoritative sources and continuity when providers or trading relationships change.

EPCIS is not required by the Regulation. An EPCIS repository is not the battery passport, although event-derived information may feed a passport where Article 77 and Annex XIII require it. EPCIS supports visibility and evidence management; it does not replace conformity assessment, audit, verification, certification or an authority decision.

19.11 GDSN and master-data exchange ​

Master data describe relatively stable attributes of a battery model, party or location; event data record what happened to identified objects. An implementation may use GDSN for master data and EPCIS for event data. GDSN supports standardised trade-item master-data exchange between trading partners, while EPCIS supports visibility event exchange. One does not replace the other.

Where GDSN is considered, the data source should identify whether each attribute describes the battery or a host product, the responsible data owner, target market, units, conditions and validation rules. The GTIN should identify the relevant trade item. A battery model and host-product model require separate identification where each is a trade item; their relationship should be explicit.

The controlled source set does not establish that current GDSN attributes and target-market profiles cover every data element required by the Regulation.

19.12 Data quality, access and confidentiality ​

Traceability data should be sufficiently accurate, complete, timely and consistent for its function. Controls should cover identifier validity, model and quantity reconciliation, duplicate events, late data, corrections, master-data changes and preservation. The GS1 Global Traceability Standard addresses data sources, precision, sensitivity, quality and sharing models in sections 3.3.1–3.3.5, pp. 18–21.

Access should be based on role and purpose. An Article 46 record must be available to a market surveillance authority on request, but Article 46 does not require it to be public or prescribe a network architecture. Commercial, authority, passport and public access should be configured separately. Applicable data-protection, confidentiality, cybersecurity and contractual requirements are not displaced by a GS1 standard.

For passport-scope batteries, Articles 77(5)–(6) and 78 separately require open standards, interoperable and machine-readable formats, an open interoperable data-exchange network, and role-based access and update rights. The use of open, interoperable and machine-readable standards can also be applied to other types of batteries

19.13 Recall and withdrawal ​

Manufacturers and importers that consider or have reason to believe that a battery they placed on the market is non-compliant shall immediately take the corrective action necessary to bring it into conformity, withdraw it or recall it, as appropriate. Where the battery presents a risk, they shall immediately inform the relevant market surveillance authority, including details of the non-compliance and corrective action (Articles 38(9) and 41(6)). Distributors shall ensure that the appropriate action is taken and have a corresponding authority-notification duty where a battery presents a risk (Article 42(5)).

Traceability records may help determine affected models, batches or instances; identify economic-operator recipients and quantities; locate stock; notify trading partners; reconcile returns; and preserve an auditable response record.

Article 46 does not universally require batch or serial data however, if records only exist at model level, the affected scope may need to include all units of that model supplied during the relevant period. More granular identification is recommended as it can narrow an action only where reliable evidence confines both the issue and distribution to that batch or those instances.
