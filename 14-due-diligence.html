14. Battery due diligence ​

14.1 Scope and applicable economic operators ​

Chapter VII applies to an economic operator that places batteries on the market or puts them into service, subject to Article 47. It is not limited to batteries requiring a battery passport. A covered operator must establish and implement a battery due-diligence policy addressing the raw materials and associated risks in Annex X and meet Articles 48–52 (Articles 47 and 48(1); Annex X, points 1–2).

Annex X lists cobalt, natural graphite, lithium, nickel and chemical compounds based on them that are necessary for manufacturing battery active materials. Its risk categories cover environment, climate and human health; human rights, labour rights and industrial relations; and community life, including that of indigenous peoples (Annex X, points 1–2). Article 48(8) permits amendments to these lists, the listed international instruments and specified obligations, so implementers need to check applicable delegated acts.

The controlled base text gives a date in Article 48(1), but Regulation (EU) 2025/1561 amended the timetable. Because the authentic amending act is not yet in the controlled source set, this guideline does not state the current operative date pending OQ-REG-009. That gap must be resolved before publication or calculation of an implementation deadline.

14.2 Turnover and circular-operation exclusions ​

Article 47 excludes an operator only where both elements of the turnover test are satisfied: it had net turnover below EUR 40 million in the financial year preceding the last financial year, and it is not part of a parent-and-subsidiary group whose consolidated turnover exceeds EUR 40 million. The operator should retain evidence for both elements.

Chapter VII also does not apply to an operator in relation to batteries subjected to preparation for re-use, preparation for repurposing, repurposing or remanufacturing where those batteries had already been placed on the market or put into service before that operation (Article 47). This is an exclusion for those batteries, not a general exemption for every battery or activity of the operator.

The exclusions do not depend on category, capacity or passport necessity. A portable or SLI battery may be within a covered operator's policy although it does not require an Article 77 passport; an industrial battery above 2 kWh may fall within the circular-operation exclusion although it requires a passport. See Section 3.4, “Determine whether a battery passport is required”, and Section 13, “Labelling, QR codes and battery passports”. Chapter VII also applies without prejudice to Union due-diligence rules for minerals and metals from conflict-affected and high-risk areas (Article 47).

14.3 Due-diligence policy ​

Article 49(1)(a)–(b) requires the covered operator to adopt and clearly communicate to suppliers and the public a company battery due-diligence policy concerning the Annex X raw materials and associated risks. The policy must incorporate standards consistent with the internationally recognised instruments in Annex X, point 4.

The policy is an operator-level framework, but its scope must be relatable to the batteries concerned because Article 52(3) requires the annual report to identify them clearly. An implementation may maintain controlled relationships between each applicable battery model or justified battery family, its material composition and supplier network, the relevant policy version, risk assessment, mitigation plan, verification evidence and public report. The Regulation prescribes neither a GS1 identifier nor a database architecture for this relationship.

Article 48(4) permits collaboration with other actors, including through due-diligence schemes recognised under the Regulation, without removing individual responsibility. Recognition is granted by the Commission under Article 53; participation in an industry scheme should not be described as equivalent unless the relevant Commission recognition applies.

14.4 Management system ​

The covered operator must structure its internal management system to support the policy, assign oversight to top management and keep records of that system for at least ten years (Article 49(1)(c)). It must also:

  • operate supply-chain controls and transparency, including a chain-of-custody or traceability system identifying upstream actors (Article 49(1)(d));
  • incorporate the policy and risk-management measures into supplier contracts and agreements (Article 49(1)(e)); and
  • establish grievance, early-warning risk-awareness and remediation mechanisms, or provide them through permitted collaborative or external arrangements based on the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (Article 49(1)(f)).

The system should distinguish legal entities, functions and physical locations; materials and material lots; batteries at the relevant identification level; documents, assessments and policy versions; and the relationships among them. This supports control and retrieval but does not add mandatory data fields beyond Articles 49–52.

14.7 Risk identification and assessment ​

The covered operator must identify and assess adverse-impact risks in its supply chain against its policy and Annex X risk categories. The assessment forms part of its management plan and uses Article 49 information together with other relevant public or stakeholder information (Article 50(1)(a)). Article 50(3) also requires assessment of the probability of adverse impacts within its risk-management system and third-party verification of its own due-diligence chains by a notified body.

Risk-assessment findings must be reported to the assigned top-management level (Article 50(1)(b)(i) and (4)). Traceability data may locate materials, actors, transactions and affected production, but the risk conclusion remains an assessment under the policy and Annex X instruments. Neither a GS1 identifier nor an EPCIS event assigns risk or demonstrates adequacy of the assessment.

14.8 Risk mitigation and monitoring ​

Article 50(1)(b) requires a strategy to prevent, mitigate and otherwise address identified impacts. It includes measures consistent with Annex X, point 4; consideration of influence over suppliers and steps to exert pressure where necessary; a risk-management plan; monitoring and tracking; reporting to top management; and consideration of suspending or discontinuing supplier engagement after failed mitigation. Additional assessments are required for risks needing mitigation or after changed circumstances (Article 50(1)(b)(ii)–(iv)).

Where mitigation continues during trade or trade is temporarily suspended, the operator must consult suppliers and relevant stakeholders before establishing measurable mitigation. These include relevant authorities, international or civil-society organisations and affected third parties such as local communities (Article 50(2)). Operational records support management and auditability but do not replace consultation, mitigation or judgement.

14.9 Third-party verification ​

The operator must have its policy verified and periodically audited by a notified body, which provides an audit report (Article 48(2)). Verification covers the activities, processes and systems used to meet Articles 49, 50 and 52; determines conformity of due-diligence practices; includes checks and stakeholder information where relevant; identifies potential improvement; and observes the stated OECD audit principles (Article 51(1)).

The notified body records its work and outcomes in a verification report and issues an approval decision where the policy fulfils Articles 49, 50 and 52 (Article 51(2)). The operator may use supplier-verification reports to assess supplier practices, and suppliers must make the reports identified in Article 49(2)(e) available downstream (Articles 49(2) and 50(3)). Identifiers can define verification scope, but do not determine conformity or replace verification, approval or audit.

14.10 Annual public reporting ​

The covered operator must review annually and publish, including online, a report on its policy. It must be readily comprehensible to end-users and clearly identify the batteries concerned. It must describe steps taken under Articles 49 and 50, significant adverse impacts and their treatment, and summarise Article 51 verification, including the notified body's name, subject to business confidentiality and competitive concerns. Where relevant, it also addresses access to environmental information, public participation and access to justice relating to sourcing, processing and trade in battery raw materials (Article 52(3)). Where the operator demonstrates that Annex X materials derive from recycled sources, it must publicly disclose its conclusions in reasonable detail, subject to the same protections (Article 52(4)).

14.13 How EPCIS may support event-based evidence ​

Articles 49–50 require documented supply-chain controls, transaction visibility and monitoring. They do not prescribe EPCIS. An implementation may use EPCIS for visibility events concerning identified materials, batteries, logistic units, parties and locations. EPCIS captures and shares those visibility events.

Events may record receiving a material lot, transforming inputs into a battery batch, aggregation, shipping, receiving or association with a host product. EPCIS expresses “what”, “when”, “where”, “why” and, where used, “how” dimensions, while master data and linked documents provide context (GS1 EPCIS Standard, version 2.0.1, sections 6.1 and 7.2.2, pp. 33–36 and 47–51). Transformation and association are covered in sections 7.4.5–7.4.6 (pp. 93–102).

Design must start from Article 49–50 needs, use identifiers at the necessary evidence granularity and protect confidential and personal information. EPCIS supports event evidence; it does not prove responsible sourcing, validate documents, perform risk assessment or replace notified-body verification. See Sections 19.9, “EPCIS event data”, and 19.10, “How EPCIS is used”.

14.14 Linking reports to the batteries concerned ​

Article 52(3) requires the report to identify the batteries concerned clearly but prescribes neither an identifier nor whether one report covers one or several models. A link design should:

  1. define the reporting operator and period;
  2. identify each in-scope model or justified grouping;
  3. associate the applicable policy, evidence and verification summary;
  4. publish stable version and status metadata; and
  5. maintain relationships when a GTIN, supplier, formulation, policy or report changes.

A GS1 Digital Link URI may provide web access from an identified battery to the report or a governed page linking to it. The link does not expand the report's scope and must not expose confidential evidence. A multi-GTIN report should list or resolve covered identifiers clearly; where evidence varies by batch, model-level presentation must not imply uniform provenance. For a battery incorporated in or supplied with another trade item, distinguish the battery identifier from the host-product identifier.

14.15 Limitations and unresolved questions ​

  • Current application date: Regulation (EU) 2025/1561 amended the timetable, but the authentic act is absent from the controlled source set. Resolve OQ-REG-009 and SG-REG-002 before stating the operative date.
  • QR access where no report is required: Article 13(6)(b) refers to the Article 52(3) report, but Article 47 can exclude the operator or battery. The presentation remains unresolved in OQ-REG-003.
  • Changing law: Article 48(8) permits amendments to Annex X and specified obligations. Check current delegated acts and recognised schemes.
  • No prescribed GS1 implementation: Chapter VII does not prescribe GTIN, batch/lot, serial number, GLN, GS1 Digital Link or EPCIS. Their use does not establish compliance.
  • Evidence-dependent granularity: Model level is inadequate where origin, supplier, material lot, assessment or mitigation differs. Chapter VII nevertheless creates no universal serialisation duty.
  • Verification and confidentiality: GS1 records may support retrieval, sharing and audit trails, but do not replace notified-body verification, audits, stakeholder engagement or risk management. Article 52 disclosure remains subject to confidentiality and competitive-concern protections.
